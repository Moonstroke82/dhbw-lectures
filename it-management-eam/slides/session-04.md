# What IT Controlling Is For {.section}

Planning, steering and transparency

---

# Goals and Tasks of IT Controlling

::: cards
### Transparency
Make IT costs and IT performance visible: what does IT cost, and what does it deliver?

### Steering
Plan budgets, prioritise projects, track progress and benefits, intervene when needed.

### Value
Ensure that IT investments deliver business value — the **efficiency** and the **effectiveness** of IT.
:::

::: callout
IT controlling supports the CIO and management: it does not decide, it provides the **information for decisions**.
:::

::: source
Based on Krcmar (2015) and Tiemeyer (2023).
:::

::: notes
Efficiency = doing things right (cost per service), effectiveness = doing the right things (contribution to business goals). IT controlling covers the whole lifecycle: portfolio (which projects?), projects (on time and budget?), operations (cost and quality of services?), and products/services.
:::

# IT Costs, Budgeting and Chargeback {.section}

Where the money goes

---

# Structure of IT Costs

| View | Categories | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cost type | Personnel, hardware, software and licences, external services, cloud | Cloud subscription for the CRM |
| Run vs. change | Running existing IT vs. changing it (projects, new functions) | Typical split: most of the budget goes into "run" |
| Capex vs. opex | Investments that are capitalised vs. ongoing expenses | Own data centre (capex) vs. cloud (opex) |
| Service view | Cost per IT service or product | Cost per workplace, per SAP user |

::: source
Own compilation based on Krcmar (2015) and Tiemeyer (2023).
:::

::: notes
The run/change split is a key steering figure: if "run" grows every year, less money is left for innovation. Cloud shifts costs from capex to opex — a frequent argument in sourcing decisions (session 15). Ask: which costs are easy to assign to a business unit, which are not (e.g. network, security)?
:::

---

# Charging IT Costs to the Business

| Model | How it works | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| No chargeback (cost centre) | IT is a central cost; business units pay nothing directly | Simple, but IT looks "free" — demand grows |
| Allocation | IT costs distributed by a key (headcount, revenue) | Simple, but little link to actual use |
| Chargeback by service | Business units pay prices per service (per user, per ticket, per GB) | Cost awareness, but effort and disputes about prices |
| Profit centre | IT sells services, possibly also to external customers | Market logic — but risk of local optimisation |

::: source
Based on Krcmar (2015) and Ross et al. (1999).
:::

::: notes
Ross, Vitale and Beath studied chargeback in ten organisations. Only in four did chargeback actually influence business managers' IT decisions — and those firms used chargeback to build communication and a shared understanding of costs and benefits between IT and the business. Message: the technical model matters less than how it is used.
:::

# IT Project Portfolio Management {.section}

Doing the right projects

---

# Why Manage IT Projects as a Portfolio?

- There are always more project ideas than budget and people
- Projects compete for the same resources — each must be judged **relative to the others**
- A portfolio balances **risk and return**, like a financial portfolio

::: callout
Portfolio management asks "**are we doing the right projects?**"; project management asks "**are we doing the projects right?**"
:::

::: source
Based on McFarlan (1981) and Krcmar (2015).
:::

::: notes
McFarlan transferred the portfolio idea from finance to IT projects: a company should not judge each project only on its own risk, but look at the risk profile of the whole set of projects. Projects differ in size, experience with the technology, and structure — all drivers of risk.
:::

---

# The Portfolio Process

::: layers
- 1 · Collect: Project proposals from business and IT with a standard business case template
- 2 · Evaluate: Score each proposal on strategic fit, value, risk, cost and dependencies
- 3 · Prioritise and decide: Rank and select within budget and capacity — e.g. in the IT steering committee
- 4 · Steer: Track running projects; stop or change projects that no longer pay off
- 5 · Review benefits: After go-live, check whether the promised benefits were realised
:::

::: source
Own illustration based on Krcmar (2015) and Tiemeyer (2023).
:::

::: notes
Step 4 is the hardest in practice: stopping projects is politically difficult ("sunk costs"). Step 5 is often skipped — and without it, business cases have no consequences, which encourages overstated benefits (next slides). Typical visualisation: a bubble chart with value vs. risk, bubble size = cost.
:::

# Business Cases {.section}

Justifying IT investments

---

# Building a Business Case

- **Costs:** one-off (project, licences, hardware, training) and running (operation, support, licences)
- **Benefits:** quantified where possible — and with an **owner** in the business who is responsible for realising them
- **Methods:** payback period, net present value (NPV), return on investment (ROI)
- **Risks and assumptions:** what must be true for the benefits to happen?

::: callout
Ward et al. found that many organisations do not demand rigorous evidence for benefits — so benefits are **overstated** and projects **oversold**.
:::

::: source
Ward et al. (2008).
:::

::: notes
Ward, Daniel and Peppard surveyed over 100 European organisations. Their recommendations: distinguish types of benefit, give each benefit an owner, link benefits to the business changes needed to achieve them, and require evidence for the expected size. Point out: IT alone rarely creates benefits — the business must change its processes.
:::

---

# Types of Benefit {.optional}

| Benefit | Example | How to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Lower licence costs, fewer staff hours | Money directly |
| Quantifiable | Faster order processing, fewer errors | Numbers, converted to money with assumptions |
| Measurable | Customer satisfaction | Measured, but not convertible to money |
| Observable | Better collaboration | Only by judgement of agreed observers |

::: source
Based on Ward et al. (2008).
:::

::: notes
Self-study. Ward et al. argue that business cases should also show the non-financial benefits honestly, instead of forcing them into money with weak assumptions.
:::

# IT KPIs and the IT Balanced Scorecard {.section}

Measuring IT performance

---

# The IT Balanced Scorecard

| Perspective | Question | Example KPIs |
|---|---|---|
| Business contribution | How does management see IT? | Share of IT projects with realised benefits, IT cost per revenue |
| User orientation | How do users see IT? | User satisfaction, SLA compliance |
| Operational excellence | How efficient are IT processes? | Incidents solved in time, project delivery on time and budget |
| Future orientation | Is IT ready for future challenges? | Training days per IT employee, share of budget for innovation |

::: source
Adapted from Kaplan and Norton (1992) and De Haes et al. (2020).
:::

::: notes
Kaplan and Norton's balanced scorecard combines financial and non-financial measures in four perspectives. Van Grembergen and colleagues adapted it to IT. The perspectives are linked by cause and effect: skilled IT staff (future orientation) → better processes (operational excellence) → satisfied users (user orientation) → business value (business contribution). Verify chapter/page in De Haes et al. before using it in written material.
:::

---

# Good KPIs {.optional}

- **Linked to a goal:** every KPI answers a question from the strategy or the scorecard
- **Measurable and comparable:** clear definition, source and frequency
- **Few:** a handful per perspective, not hundreds
- **Balanced:** not only costs — a cheap IT that delivers no value is no success
- **Owned:** someone is responsible for each KPI and its target

::: source
Own summary based on Kaplan and Norton (1992) and Tiemeyer (2023).
:::

::: notes
Self-study. Common trap: measuring what is easy (number of tickets) instead of what matters (business processes running without disruption).
:::

# Exercise: Build an IT Business Case {.exercise}

**Case:** *Rhein-Neckar Mobil Group* (session 3) considers replacing its three ERP systems with **one cloud ERP**.

- One-off costs: 4.0 m€ (project, data migration, training)
- Running costs today: 1.8 m€ per year for the three systems; after migration: 1.2 m€ per year
- Expected process benefits: 0.5 m€ per year from fewer manual reconciliations (from year 2)

**Tasks:**
1. Calculate the **payback period** (simple, without discounting)
2. List three **benefits** that are not in the numbers and classify them (financial, quantifiable, measurable, observable)
3. Name two **risks** and the **assumptions** behind the benefit figures
4. Who should be the **benefit owner**? Which **KPIs** would you track after go-live?

::: notes
Groups of 4–5, about 30 min + 10 min discussion.
Solution for 1: annual savings year 1 = 1.8 − 1.2 = 0.6 m€; from year 2: 0.6 + 0.5 = 1.1 m€ per year. Cumulative: year 1 0.6, year 2 1.7, year 3 2.8, year 4 3.9, year 5 5.0 → payback during year 5 (about 4.1 years). Discuss: an NPV calculation would make the result worse (later savings are worth less).
2 e.g. one data basis for the joint mobility app (observable/measurable), faster month-end closing (quantifiable), lower security risk through a standard system (observable). 3 Risks: migration delays, business units resist harmonised processes; assumptions: running cost of the cloud ERP stays at 1.2 m€ (licence price increases!), reconciliation savings are real and staff time is actually reduced. 4 Benefit owner: CFO or heads of the business units, not the CIO. KPIs: closing days, reconciliation hours, running IT cost, user satisfaction.
:::

# Key Takeaways

- **IT controlling** creates transparency on IT costs and performance and supports decisions
- IT costs can be viewed by cost type, **run vs. change**, capex vs. opex and per service
- **Chargeback** can build cost awareness — if it is used for dialogue between IT and the business
- **Portfolio management** chooses the right projects; **business cases** need owned, evidenced benefits
- The **IT balanced scorecard** measures IT in four linked perspectives
